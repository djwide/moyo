No commercial passenger flights beyond the four hijacked and crashed on September 11, 2001—American Airlines Flight 11, United Airlines Flight 175, American Airlines Flight 77, and United Airlines Flight 93—were permitted to operate in U.S. airspace after 9:45 a.m. EDT.

At 9:45 a.m., the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issued a nationwide ground stop, halting all takeoffs at U.S. airports. At approximately 9:59 a.m., the FAA ordered all airborne aircraft to land at the nearest suitable airport—a directive known as a “national ground stop” and “land-and-hold-short” order. This was the first time in U.S. civil aviation history that such a complete shutdown of civilian air traffic had occurred.

By 11:08 a.m., the FAA confirmed that all 4,546 civilian aircraft then in flight over the United States had landed safely. No further scheduled or unscheduled commercial passenger flights operated in U.S. airspace for the remainder of September 11.

General aviation (private planes) and military flights were also grounded under the same orders, with limited exceptions granted only for emergency medical transport, law enforcement, and military operations—subject to explicit FAA and NORAD coordination and approval. For example, a small number of Air Force One–related support flights and medical evacuation helicopters operated under strict clearance later that day, but these were not scheduled commercial services.

The U.S. airspace remained closed to commercial traffic until 11:00 a.m. on September 13, when the FAA began permitting limited, pre-approved domestic flights to resume under enhanced security protocols.
